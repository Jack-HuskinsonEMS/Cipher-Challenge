My dear friend,

I must commend you on your taste in theatre as well as in companions. Your suggestion that I should attend Mme Bernhardt's latest triumph was well-timed, since my business had brought me to Paris and left me in need of a distraction. While the military adventure in Egypt has had many diplomatic ramifications which will be felt for some time, the impact on our relations with France is the most immediate. 

The mood here is distinctly uneasy. The understanding that our interests in that country were shared has been badly shaken, and with the two governments are rattling their sabres, I fear that matters may yet worsen. Against the ill-tempered discussions I was forced to hold with my counterparts in Paris, the time spent with Mme Bernhardt stood in marked contrast. She is a woman of grace and considerable accomplishment, and I was entranced both by her skill as an actress and by her gifts as a hostess. 

She seemed able to understand the deep concern that I know you share for the future of Anglo-French relations, and I think she will prove a very useful ally in our attempts to stabilise the situation. She clearly has the ear of many influential members of Parisian society and of the government itself, and I think we would be wise to enlist her in our efforts. 

Beyond that, I must admit my astonishment at her evident skill as a sculptress. I had heard that she was a gifted artist but had assumed this referred to her skill with brush and canvas. I don't think you had mentioned that the extraordinary bronze inkwell on your desk in the study at Marlborough House was a self-portrait rendered by her. If you had, then I had forgotten it, and I only realised the connection when she showed me a model she had made in preparation for the final object. 

The strangeness of the thing with its bat wings and sepulchral air is almost singular in its effect, and I was immediately drawn to ask her about it, whereupon she confided the connection with you. Before I conclude, I must impress upon you one matter of particular importance for the protection of us all. 

The growing ill feeling across the Channel presents both a danger and an opportunity. If we make our moves wisely, I think we may forge a powerful new alliance. A single false move, however, could cost us dearly and place our friends and allies in considerable danger. It is essential that we take care to conceal our plans, and I suggest that we ensure the secrecy of our correspondence by adopting more secure ciphers. 

If you agree, then I shall prepare a schedule of cipher keys to share with Mme Bernhardt and with you when I return from Egypt. I shall be travelling there once my business here in Paris is concluded. While there are more secure methods that I know you have been trained to use, I fear that the Divine S. B. has less experience in such matters, and so I shall recommend that we use a keyword substitution cipher for our future communications. Once she is comfortable with that system, we might perhaps move on to others that afford even greater security. 

I await your reply.

Your friend,
E. C.
